Comparing Wuhan to Chornobyl

November 2026
This blog post was written with Claude: the argument was developed in conversation, Claude produced the text. I use the Ukrainian spelling Chornobyl except where quoting a source.

Thirty percent

In February 2020, five virologists wrote the paper that settled the public question of where COVID-19 came from. “The Proximal Origin of SARS-CoV-2” told the world that no laboratory scenario was plausible, and for the next three years anyone who raised one was arguing against the scientific consensus.

Their private Slack messages, released this year, show the lead author putting a lab origin at roughly thirty percent while they drafted it. A co-author was at ten. One of them had written days earlier that accidental release was not a fringe theory but in fact highly likely.

I want to be careful about what that does and does not show. It is not evidence that the virus came from a lab. Thirty percent is not knowledge; it is uncertainty, and uncertainty is the honest state to have been in during February 2020. What it shows is a gap between what a group of experts believed and what they told the public, and that gap is the thing I find worth writing about. This post is not about where the virus came from. It is about how institutions behave when they do not know, and what that behaviour costs.

Not engineered, not exonerated

First, the part that is actually settled. The virus was not built. The features once offered as fingerprints of engineering — the furin cleavage site, the pattern of restriction sites — have not survived contact with the bat sarbecoviruses sequenced since, and reanalyses using those newer genomes find no anomaly to explain. Anyone still claiming a designed bioweapon is arguing against the sequence data, not from it.

That conclusion is narrower than it sounds, and the narrowness is the whole problem. A wild sarbecovirus collected in Yunnan, carried to Wuhan and stored in a freezer is still a wild sarbecovirus if it gets out. It would look in every respect like one that came off a market stall, because it would be the same kind of object. The genome cannot distinguish a market from a laboratory, so it was never going to settle anything. What could have settled it was paperwork: sampling records, freezer inventories, staff health logs, the institute’s sequence database.

Clearing the market

None of that paperwork has ever been produced, and the first thing that happened was the removal of the one piece of physical evidence anyone could have examined.

On 1 January 2020, hours after the Huanan Seafood Wholesale Market was closed, a team from the Chinese CDC arrived to swab it. The animals had already been taken away. The investigators sampled floors, stalls, carts and drains in a market whose occupants were gone, and the species everyone would spend the next six years arguing about were never tested at all. The Wuhan Institute of Virology’s sequence database went offline in September 2019 and has not been reopened to outsiders. Doctors in Wuhan who raised the alarm in late December were reprimanded.

The result is on the record. The WHO’s own advisory group reported in 2025 that it could neither investigate a laboratory origin nor rule one out, because China never supplied the sequences or the biosecurity records. Note what kind of failure that is. It is not that the evidence was examined and came out ambiguous. The evidence was never handed over, by the only party holding it, and that party is also the only one who already knows the answer.

Vienna, twice

Chornobyl ran the same playbook, and comparing them shows which parts of it work.

The first move is denial, and at Chornobyl it lasted about two days — not because the Soviet state was any less inclined to conceal, but because caesium from an open reactor core blew across a border and set off a detector at a Swedish power station, and Sweden said it would go to the IAEA. The evidence left the jurisdiction of the party with an interest in silence, and it arrived legible. Wuhan had no equivalent. A virus crosses borders without saying where it started.

The second move is minimisation, and there the Soviet state did far better. At the IAEA’s Vienna meeting that August, Valery Legasov presented an account in which the helicopter drops had smothered the burning core, when the release data from the following days showed they had not. Pages on fallout over Belarus were cut from the report shortly beforehand. Legasov told the Soviet Academy of Sciences afterwards that he had not lied at Vienna but had not told the whole truth, and killed himself two years to the day after the accident. That sentence is the epitaph for this entire genre of institutional behaviour.

The third move is the one I keep returning to, because it does not require a state at all. The IAEA was charged with promoting nuclear power and regulating its safety at the same time, and researchers who later worked inside Unit 4 argue its response was shaped by that double mandate: conceding that the famous containment operation had failed would have discredited the agency’s own endorsements and an industry already bruised by Three Mile Island. The body best placed to investigate had a stake in the finding.

Virology’s version was less formal and therefore harder to see. The February 2020 Lancet statement condemning lab-origin “conspiracy theories” was drafted at EcoHealth Alliance — which funded coronavirus work at the Wuhan institute — and deliberately arranged to read as an independent letter from leading scientists, with several signatories’ ties to that organisation left undisclosed. The IAEA’s conflict was written into its charter, where anyone could point at it. This one was shared funding and collegial loyalty among the few hundred people qualified to judge the question, which is a conflict nobody in the room can see because everyone in the room has it.

Four thousand and fifteen million

Here is where the comparison stops being a parallel and starts being an indictment.

Chornobyl is the disaster everyone reaches for, and it is by a wide margin the smaller of the two. The Chernobyl Forum projected around four thousand eventual deaths; higher estimates are contested and run from tens of thousands upward, and UNSCEAR’s position is that the long-term figure is simply uncertain. The WHO puts COVID-19’s excess mortality for its first two years alone at about fifteen million.

So the larger catastrophe is also the one whose cause was never established, and the consequences of that are not academic. Chornobyl was attributed, and attribution produced reform: the reactor design was revised, and five months after the accident the IAEA adopted a convention obliging states to notify one another of nuclear accidents. The detail I find most instructive is that the Soviet Union signed it at the conference and ratified quickly — the state that had just spent five months concealing an accident put its name to the mechanism that would have exposed it. Wuhan was never attributed, so neither candidate cause was reformed. The wildlife trade continues and the laboratories continue, each sheltered by the live possibility that it was the other one.

And the cover-up may well have protected a laboratory that an honest investigation would have cleared. Most virologists hold that the balance of evidence favours an animal origin; the intelligence assessments that lean the other way do so at low confidence. If the institute had nothing to do with it, then the suppression bought it nothing and cost it everything, because an institution that cannot be investigated can never be exonerated either. Public confidence in scientists has fallen substantially since early 2020, and what it tracks is the handling, not any finding about the virus.

That is the part that should worry anyone thinking about the next one. Thirty percent was a defensible thing to believe in February 2020. Telling the public it was zero was not, and six years later the bill for that decision is still larger than the risk it was meant to insure against.
