Comparing Wuhan to Chornobyl

November 2026
This blog post was developed in conversation with Claude and revised with Codex. I use the Ukrainian spelling Chornobyl except where quoting a source.

Thirty percent

On 17 March 2020, five scientists published “The Proximal Origin of SARS-CoV-2”. Its conclusion included a striking sentence: “we do not believe that any type of laboratory-based scenario is plausible.” The paper also acknowledged that some origin theories could not then be proved or disproved, and that further evidence could change the assessment. That tension between a firm dismissal and an unresolved question is where this comparison begins.

Private Slack messages released by Senator Rand Paul in July 2026 include an estimate by lead author Kristian Andersen putting a laboratory origin at thirty percent. That is a subjective judgement, not a measured probability. Nor does a private estimate, by itself, prove that an author knowingly misled readers: beliefs can change, and the timing and context of each exchange matter.

What interests me is the obligation that comes with speaking authoritatively under uncertainty. Scientists should explain what supports their conclusions, what remains unresolved, and what evidence could change their minds. Institutions should make that evidence available for independent scrutiny. When they fail to do so, they obstruct both accountability and learning. That is the reason I want to compare Wuhan with Chornobyl.

What the genome can tell us

There is a distinction between how a virus evolved and how it first infected a human. Evidence against genetic engineering does not, by itself, exclude an accident involving a naturally evolved virus. Equally, the possibility of such an accident is not evidence that one occurred.

Genomic evidence matters. A 2025 reanalysis of alleged engineering signatures in restriction-site patterns found those patterns unremarkable when compared with a broader set of related viruses. That undermines a particular argument for engineering; it does not reconstruct the virus’s entire history.

A naturally evolved virus could, in principle, infect someone through contact with wildlife or through work with a collected sample. Distinguishing those routes requires genomic and epidemiological evidence alongside records of animal movements, sampling, laboratory holdings and staff illnesses. Some of the most useful evidence may exist only in records held by institutions with a stake in the investigation.

The missing evidence

Some opportunities to gather evidence were lost early. When Chinese CDC investigators began sampling the Huanan market on 1 January 2020, the animals had already been removed. Swabs from stalls, cages and drains still yielded information, but they could not replace testing the live animals that had been sold there. Closing a market during an outbreak can be a public-health measure while also making its origins harder to investigate. That consequence alone does not establish an intention to conceal.

Other gaps concern information that was requested but not shared. In its June 2025 assessment, the WHO’s advisory group concluded that the weight of available evidence favoured transmission from animals to humans, directly from bats or through an intermediate host. WHO also reported that China had not supplied requested early genetic sequences, fuller information about animals sold in Wuhan’s markets, or information about research and biosafety conditions in Wuhan’s laboratories.

Both findings matter. The available evidence can favour one explanation while important questions remain unresolved. Withholding records does not tell us what those records contain, or whether their holders know how the pandemic began. It prevents outsiders from assessing them. The case for disclosure does not depend on assuming the answer in advance.

Evidence beyond state control

Chornobyl shows what changes when evidence becomes available outside the control of the state being questioned. On 28 April 1986, two days after the explosion, radiation detected at Forsmark in Sweden alerted investigators to fallout from abroad. The Soviet authorities could control their own announcements, but they could not control measurements made in another country.

There is a limit to the comparison. Detecting a reactor release is different from identifying how a virus first reached humans. The international spread of COVID-19 made the outbreak visible around the world, but did not identify the route of the first human infection. A virus crosses borders without saying where it started.

Nor did detecting Chornobyl’s fallout immediately explain the accident. The IAEA’s 1992 reassessment revised the earlier emphasis on operator error, giving greater weight to reactor design and failures in the safety system. It identified an emergency-shutdown defect omitted from the initial Soviet account. Evidence of a disaster and evidence of responsibility for it are different things; the latter can remain incomplete long after the former is undeniable.

That is the useful parallel. Independent measurements can challenge an official account, but understanding which safeguards failed also requires access to records and scrutiny of the institutions that produced them.

A stake in the answer

The people best qualified to assess a failure may also have professional relationships with those being assessed. Expertise is necessary, but it does not remove the need to disclose those relationships.

The February 2020 Lancet statement supporting scientists in China condemned “conspiracy theories suggesting that COVID-19 does not have a natural origin.” Its signatories included EcoHealth Alliance president Peter Daszak, and it declared no competing interests. In June 2021, The Lancet published an expanded disclosure describing his organisation’s research collaborations in China, including with the Wuhan Institute of Virology.

Those relationships do not establish that the statement’s scientific conclusion was wrong. They were nevertheless relevant to how readers should assess its independence. Supporting colleagues facing abuse is compatible with asking for their records to be examined. Treating those commitments as incompatible makes institutional loyalty an obstacle to investigation.

The cost of not knowing

The comparison matters because understanding a disaster helps us prevent the next one. After Chornobyl, the reactor design was revised and an international convention established obligations to report nuclear accidents with potential cross-border consequences. The Soviet Union itself signed: the state that had concealed the accident accepted a mechanism intended to make future concealment harder.

COVID-19 leaves us with unresolved questions about both wildlife trade and laboratory safety. We can improve both without settling the origin question. But withholding evidence makes it harder to identify which safeguards failed, who should answer for those failures, and what specifically needs to change.

Secrecy also harms the institutions it is supposed to protect. An open investigation might have cleared the Wuhan Institute of Virology, or substantially reduced suspicion of it. If it had nothing to do with the outbreak, access to relevant records would have offered a way to test that account. Withholding them leaves both accusations and denials harder to assess.

That brings me back to the thirty percent. Scientists were entitled to uncertainty in 2020, and the public was entitled to an honest account of it. A conclusion can be the best explanation available without being a settled answer. Preparing for the next pandemic requires institutions willing to disclose what they know, acknowledge what they do not, and allow both to be independently examined.
